Lessons on Food & Beverage Profitability: Private Clubs vs. Public Venues

Published November 2026 • By Max Moreno, CCM, CCE

Few topics spark more debate in boardrooms and executive suites than Food & Beverage profitability. Across hospitality, a public restaurant is built to maximize seat turns, capture transient demand, and optimize prime cost for standalone profit. A private club or luxury resort dining room operates under a fundamentally different economic mandate.

When boards evaluate club F&B strictly through a commercial restaurant lens, they often miss what actually drives long-term club health—while overlooking real operational leaks that can and should be fixed. Having led multi-amenity operations and executed financial turnarounds that cut annual operating expenses by 14% while growing F&B revenue by 18% in year one, here are the core lessons I apply when comparing private club F&B against public hospitality.

1. Profit Center vs. Value & Retention Engine

In public hospitality, every cover is a transactional customer acquisition and margin event. In a private club, members are recurring stakeholders whose dues, initiation fees, and long-term retention anchor the broader enterprise. Industry benchmarking consistently shows that F&B carries massive weight in overall member satisfaction—often outranking single amenities—yet many top-performing clubs run deliberate F&B deficits when investing in member experience. The executive job is not chasing artificial break-even at the expense of belonging; it is maximizing net contribution and member value together.

2. Predictable Demand Peaks vs. High Table Turns

Public restaurants thrive on steady walk-in traffic and multiple nightly table turns. Private clubs face concentrated demand spikes—Friday nights, weekend golf waves, seasonal shifts, and banquet calendars—followed by quieter mid-week windows.

  • Public model: High volume velocity, standardized high-margin menus, and aggressive turn management.
  • Private club model: Relationship-driven dining, personalized preferences, and dynamic labor scheduling tied closely to tee sheets, event calendars, and seasonal flow.

3. Hospitality Generosity vs. Cost & Pour Discipline

In private clubs, hospitality culture naturally leans toward warmth, familiarity, and generous member touches—which can quietly erode margins if left unchecked through heavy pours, unrecorded comps, inconsistent portioning, or bloated menus. Public venues enforce tighter front-of-house and back-of-house cost controls by necessity.

The lesson from my own turnaround work is that discipline and warmth are not opposites. Tightening procurement agreements, aligning prep and labor with real demand curves, engineering focused menus, and coaching teams on consistent execution protect margins while elevating perceived quality.

4. Measuring What Actually Moves the Needle

Evaluating a private club dining room solely on standalone restaurant ratios tells only part of the story. Strong executive leadership pairs disciplined cost-of-goods, labor productivity, and per-cover/event contribution metrics with member retention, dining satisfaction, and overall club financial health.

The Convergence of Luxury Hospitality: Modern Senior Living vs. Today’s Private Club

Published November 2026 • By Max Moreno, CCM, CCE

The traditional boundaries between premier private clubs and modern senior residential communities have largely disappeared. Today’s active adult and luxury wellness communities no longer resemble institutional care facilities—they operate like high-touch private clubs with residential living at their core.

Having led complex, multi-amenity private clubs and destination properties, I see firsthand why executive leadership skills transfer seamlessly between both sectors. At their core, both models succeed or fail on the same four pillars.

1. Recurring Stakeholder Relationships Over Transactional Visits

Unlike hotels or public restaurants that constantly chase transient guests, both private clubs and residential wellness communities serve a stable, recurring population. Residents and members expect staff to know their names, dietary preferences, routines, and milestones. Long-term retention, word-of-mouth referrals, and emotional belonging—not one-off transactions—drive enterprise value.

2. Multi-Amenity Lifestyle & Culinary Excellence

Modern retirement communities compete on the exact same lifestyle amenities that define top country clubs: chef-driven dining venues, curated wellness and fitness programming, social clubs, concierge services, and impeccably maintained grounds. In both environments, Food & Beverage is the social heartbeat of the community—requiring thoughtful menu variety, nutrition-conscious customization, and disciplined cost control.

3. Governance, Stewardship & Capital Asset Protection

Whether reporting to an equity club board, a member advisory committee, or an ownership/operating group, the General Manager or COO must balance daily hospitality with long-range asset stewardship. Managing preventative maintenance, capital reserves, regulatory compliance, and vendor contracts ensures physical facilities remain safe, reliable, and competitive across decades.

4. Purpose-Driven Culture & High-Touch Service Standards

Both environments rely on empowering large, multi-departmental teams to deliver warmth, dignity, and consistency every single day. Proven service frameworks—built on clear standards, visible floor leadership, and genuine care—turn routine operational touchpoints into meaningful human connections.

As demographics evolve and expectations for active, wellness-focused living rise, the leaders who thrive in modern senior living are those who bring private-club hospitality, commercial discipline, and authentic community building together.

Beyond the Hotel Model: Why Private Club Hospitality Demands a Higher Standard

Published November 2026 • By Max Moreno, CCM, CCE

In hospitality, hotels and private clubs are often grouped together—yet operationally, culturally, and emotionally, they operate on entirely different planes. While luxury hotels excel at orchestrating memorable short-term stays for transient guests, premier private clubs master something far more demanding: delivering effortless, deeply personalized hospitality to the exact same community week after week, year after year.

That recurring relationship changes everything. When you serve the same members and their families across decades, you cannot rely on scripted smiles or novelty. Every interaction is cumulative, and any gap in consistency is immediately noticed.

1. Recurring Members vs. Transient Guests

A hotel typically has three to five days to impress a guest before check-out resets the clock. In a private club, there is no reset button. Members own a stake in the culture, know the property intimately, and hold institutional memory. Because they return constantly, they quickly spot inconsistencies—and rightly expect the club to evolve alongside their families. Earning trust under that level of daily scrutiny requires genuine excellence rather than temporary polish.

2. Seamless Consistency Across Every Department Transition

In a hotel, departments often operate as parallel amenities. In a premier private club, a member’s afternoon flows seamlessly from the golf shop or tennis courts to the locker room, the terrace grill, and an evening wine dinner. True club hospitality demands unified communication across every transition so the member never feels like a stranger moving between silos—their preferences, milestones, and expectations travel with them automatically.

3. True Anticipation Over Reactive Service

Hotel service is largely built around responding well when a guest asks for something. Private club hospitality is built on knowing before they ask—from how a member prefers their beverage prepared to seating preferences, dietary needs, and family traditions. That level of anticipation only happens when leadership embeds structured observation, shared member intelligence, and proactive daily lineups into the operational rhythm.

4. Training for Genuine Warmth—Never Servitude

Perhaps the greatest distinction lies in how teams are trained. Scripted hotel protocols can easily slip into mechanical compliance or hollow servitude. In top private clubs, we train professionals to bring authentic warmth, poise, and mutual respect to every encounter—pairing rigorous technical standards with genuine human connection. When staff feel proud of their craft and respected as hospitality professionals, members feel truly cared for rather than merely served.

That is why private club leadership remains the pinnacle of hospitality management: it demands operational precision, emotional intelligence, and unwavering consistency every single day.

Modernizing Private Club Governance: Why Legacy Standing Committees Stall Strategic Momentum

Published November 2026 • By Max Moreno, CCM, CCE

For decades, private clubs operated on a familiar governance blueprint: an array of standing departmental committees—Greens, Golf, Aquatics, Food & Beverage, House, and Finance—paired with a one-year rotating Club President. Historically, that model served as a proving ground for future board members and gave constituents a direct voice in daily operations. Today, however, that traditional hierarchy has become one of the single greatest bottlenecks to club vibrancy, capital execution, and long-term relevance.

Having led premier private clubs and complex multi-amenity properties through major capital renovations, operational turnarounds, and multi-year strategic master planning, I have seen firsthand how legacy governance structures collide with the speed of modern business. To remain competitive, private clubs must shift from micromanaging operational committees to strategic, agile governance.

1. The Pitfalls of Standing Departmental Committees

Modern hospitality moves too fast for monthly committee debates over menu items, bunker sand, or pool hours. When standing committees sit permanently over operational departments, three predictable failures emerge:

  • Single-Issue Agendas & Constituent Politics: Permanent seats frequently attract vocal individuals lobbying for narrow personal preferences rather than the holistic good of the club.
  • Operational Micromanagement: Well-meaning volunteers inadvertently blur the line between board governance and General Manager/COO execution—slowing down decisions, undermining professional leadership, and eroding staff accountability.
  • Strategic Paralysis: When every capital upgrade or policy shift must survive a gauntlet of overlapping committees, multi-year master plans lose momentum before a single shovel hits the ground.

2. The Agile Alternative: Purpose-Built Ad-Hoc Task Forces

Forward-thinking clubs are retiring permanent operational committees in favor of focused, time-bound Ad-Hoc Committees (Task Forces) chartered strictly around strategic initiatives—such as capital construction projects, legal or bylaw revisions, digital marketing and brand positioning, or philanthropic foundations.

The ad-hoc model works because it matches talent to the exact problem at hand:

  • Targeted Expertise: Instead of filling seats to satisfy social representation, leadership recruits members with genuine professional expertise—architects, attorneys, brand executives, or financial strategists—for that specific project.
  • Clear Scope & Automatic Dissolution: Each task force operates with a defined mandate, timeline, and deliverable. The moment the project is completed, the committee dissolves—preventing bureaucratic creep and volunteer burnout.

3. Multi-Year Presidential Terms & Executive Succession

Equally critical is rethinking executive board tenure. The traditional one-year Club President term forces a perpetual reset: by the time a President masters the strategic landscape, they are preparing to hand over the gavel. Executing a living, multi-year strategic master plan requires continuity at the top.

Best-in-class governance establishes a minimum two- to three-year Presidential term anchored by a transparent Executive Committee succession pipeline. When leaders accept a seat on the Executive Committee with the clear understanding that they are preparing to serve as Vice President and ultimately President, strategic alignment deepens, owner-board trust stabilizes, and the GM/COO has a true multi-year partner in execution.

4. Elevating the Nominating Committee to the Most Important Role in the Club

When you eliminate standing operational committees as a feeder system, the Nominating Committee becomes the single most consequential body in the club. Rather than rewarding tenure or popularity, a modern Nominating Committee acts like an executive search firm—identifying strategic thinkers, fiduciary stewards, and collaborative leaders who understand the difference between governance and management.

Challenging decades of club tradition is never comfortable, nor is it universally popular at first. Yet the clubs thriving today—and positioning themselves to lead over the next decade—are those brave enough to replace political hierarchy with strategic clarity, professional accountability, and decisive momentum.

Treating F&B as a Strategic Value Driver: Lessons From Industry Data and Club Leadership

Published November 2026 • By Max Moreno, CCM, CCE

One of the most persistent misconceptions in private club governance is judging Food & Beverage solely as a standalone profit center. Industry research highlighted in Club Benchmarking’s 2025 Food & Beverage Whitepaper by Bryan LaBlue reinforces what seasoned club operators see on the ground: F&B financial outcomes reflect deliberate strategic choices rather than a simple measure of operational competence, and stronger clubs frequently treat F&B as an experience-building amenity rather than chasing artificial dining profits.

In my own leadership experience across private clubs and multi-amenity hospitality operations, viewing F&B through a value and experience lens—paired with disciplined execution—has been a powerful catalyst for elevating how both current and prospective members perceive the club.

1. Moving Beyond the "Profit vs. Loss" Myth

Applying a commercial restaurant scorecard to a private club overlooks how a club actually creates wealth. Members join and stay for lifestyle, community, and the quality of the overall environment. When leadership frames F&B around member experience, culinary consistency, and perceived value—while still eliminating genuine waste and controlling costs—boardroom energy shifts from defensive line-item debates to strategic growth.

2. Shaping Value for Current and Prospective Members

Dining is one of the most visible daily touchpoints in a club and plays a major role in shaping overall member satisfaction and club culture. When members experience high quality, welcoming service, and strong value in the dining rooms and social spaces, two things happen:

  • Existing members feel greater pride and ownership: High-value dining and engaging social programming deepen daily usage and reinforce the return on annual dues.
  • Prospective members see unmistakable demand: A vibrant, well-attended dining and social atmosphere signals momentum and community vitality—helping attract quality applicants, strengthen initiation value, and build or sustain waitlists.

3. My Approach in Practice: Experience, Discipline, and Balance

Supporting a data-informed amenity mindset does not mean ignoring financial stewardship. In my work leading club and hospitality operations, I have used this philosophy by focusing on three practical priorities:

  • Protecting quality and consistency: Investing in kitchen reliability, preparation standards, and attentive service so members get an experience that feels special rather than rushed or penny-pinched.
  • Eliminating waste without eroding experience: Tightening purchasing, aligning staffing to actual demand patterns, and keeping menus focused so resources go toward what members genuinely value.
  • Connecting F&B to broader club health: Measuring success through member satisfaction, engagement, retention, and overall club vitality—not treating F&B in isolation from the rest of the operation.

Industry benchmarking provides vital perspective, and real leadership happens when data meets everyday execution. When a club aligns its dining strategy with member value and long-term demand, F&B becomes one of the strongest engines of community loyalty and club appeal.
